Scape Styles

Six ways to build a living landscape

Every installation in the gallery grows out of one of these traditions. Read the style, then see it built — in the gallery, and in Hakkai's own build logs.

01

Nature Aquarium

The broadest and most forgiving tradition — stone, wood and planting arranged to read as a believable slice of nature rather than a designed object. Most installations in this gallery draw from it: it rewards patience and plant growth more than any single dramatic material.

02

Iwagumi

Stone-led minimalism: an odd number of rocks, one dominant, set in careful relationship to each other, with a single low carpeting plant filling in around them. Nothing to hide behind — the layout has to be right the first time.

03

Dutch

The oldest structured style in the hobby: rows and terraces of contrasting stem plants, grown and trimmed like a garden bed rather than arranged around hardscape. No stone or wood at all — the planting does all the work.

Build stories from Hakkai

  • The Art of Dutch-Style Aquascapinghakkai.com
  • Redoing the Dutch Tank at HakkaiStory
04

Paludarium

Where the waterline stops being the edge of the design. Emersed planting, misting systems and land elements sit above a working aquarium below — the most technically demanding style here, and the one that reads least like a fish tank.

From the gallery

  • Ledgers FallInstallation
  • Compact, Lush & Full of LifeInstallation
05

Wabi Kusa

A ball or mat of moss and stem plants grown emersed until roots knit it together, then dropped into any container of water — a portable, low-commitment piece of living sculpture, and often the fastest way into the hobby.

Build stories from Hakkai

  • Wabi KusaStory
  • Bonsai Tree AquascapeStory
06

Nano & Nature

A full ecosystem compressed into a footprint small enough for a desk — the same craft as a full-size nature aquarium, just working in inches instead of feet. What it gives up in scale, it makes up in how close you can get to it.
